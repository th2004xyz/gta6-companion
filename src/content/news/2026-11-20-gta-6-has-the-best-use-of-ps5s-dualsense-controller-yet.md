## Overview

I expected Xbox Series X|S's Quick Resume to be the defining feature of this console generation, but it's been the unique features offered by PS5's DualSense controller that have actually provided the biggest edge for me. GTA 6 is not only the latest such example of this, but it's also the best example of leveraging the DualSense yet. The controller's speaker is used for some nice touches, like hearing your phone ring or chatter on the police scanner, but as is often the case, it's the DualSense triggers that make the more meaningful difference. These are leveraged in myriad ways that can significantly enhance the experience of whatever you're doing. For instance, the home-repair tasks you can complete early in the game see the triggers react differently based on what you're doing. It just feels more immersive for the triggers to not behave normally when you're installing screws with a driver; that's an action that should not feel identical to firing a gun or hitting the gas in your car. Similarly, there's an added weight to the strength-training activities you can take part in that lend an added sense that Jason and Lucia are actually picking up heavy items, not just manipulating weightless objects made of 1s and 0s. But where I think the DualSense triggers really shine is in driving. The triggers have a soft lock that allow you to pull and maintain a speed that is appropriate for the road you're on. Certainly in the early hours, when money is at a premium and alternative vehicles are not easy to steal , keeping your car in good shape is made easier by obeying traffic laws, and that is easier if you can comfortably pull the trigger without maxing out the RPMs. You can just as easily pull the trigger past that point to put the pedal to the floor and go as fast as you want, but that little bit of resistance lends that dangerous decision a bit of extra excitement, almost like a piece of physical feedback that you're choosing to break the law. Part of what makes these interactions work so well is how many years we've spent interacting with triggers on a game controller in a very narrow way: You might pull it partway, but generally you're just mashing it down into the plastic of the controller without much regard. By varying the resistance you encounter based on what you're doing, the abstraction of interacting with a video game world via a controller is pushed ever so slightly in the direction of realism. And for a game like GTA 6, where all of the minor details are what make it so notable, it's just one more way in which it feels less like a video game.
